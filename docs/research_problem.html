Research problem

Does a VAE latent space keep the loop of periodic data?

Reproducing and testing VAE latent spaces with prescribed topology, measured with persistent homology

Jules Géraud Odjé · Geometry and Topology in Machine Learning, University of Fribourg · Fall 2026 · Project 4, research direction 1

The problem

Many quantities go round in a circle: the hour of the day, an angle, a season. After 23:00 comes 00:00, and 359° sits next to 0°. Data driven by such a quantity lie on a loop.

A variational autoencoder (VAE) summarises high-dimensional data with a few hidden numbers, the latent space. In the standard VAE this space is flat: a line, a plane. A flat space cannot hold a loop without cutting it somewhere, the way a world map cuts the Pacific in two. Two points that are neighbours in reality, 23:00 and 00:00, end up far apart in the model.

Research question

When the data lie on a cylinder, does a VAE whose latent space is itself a cylinder recover the loop that a standard Gaussian VAE tears, and can persistent homology measure the difference?

The variational autoencoder

In general. An encoder compresses each observation x into a small code z, and a decoder rebuilds x from z. In a VAE the code is a small cloud rather than a point, and all clouds must fit a fixed shape, the prior. Training balances the two goals:

loss = reconstruction error + β · KL( code cloud ‖ prior ) rebuild each observation well · keep all codes arranged like the prior · β sets the trade-off

The setting of this project. We use the synthetic data of the paper. Each point has an angle θ (a circle) and a height h between 0 and 1 (an interval), so the data lie on a cylinder S¹ × [0, 1]. The cylinder is pushed through a fixed random map into 50 dimensions, with noise. The model only sees the 50 numbers and must recover the cylinder hidden inside.

x = g(cos θ, sin θ, h) + noise,  x ∈ ℝ⁵⁰ example: θ = 90°, h = 0.3 → the point (0, 1, 0.3) on the cylinder, then mapped to 50 numbers

The method

Step 1 · The standard VAE: a flat prior

The standard VAE (Kingma and Welling, 2014) uses a Gaussian prior on ℝ²: a hill whose summit is at the centre. A plane could hold a ring, but the ring needs an empty centre while the hill wants the codes at the centre. The two demands conflict, and the model compromises by opening the ring into a spiral.

Why it is not enough. In the spiral, two codes on either side of the cut are close in the latent space but far apart on the true circle, or the reverse. The loop of the data is lost.

Step 2 · Prescribed topology: give the latent space the shape of the data

Van Hulst, Tomczak, Heemels and Antunes (2026) build the latent space from elementary pieces: a circle for what goes round, an interval for what goes from one end to the other. The pieces are combined to match the data; here a circle times an interval gives a cylinder. The code cloud and the prior both live on the cylinder, and the KL term keeps a closed form.

The prior no longer has a centre to fill. The data form a loop and the prior is a loop, so the conflict of Step 1 disappears and nothing needs to be torn.

Step 3 · Measuring the loop: persistent homology

A figure shows the spiral, but a scientific comparison needs a number. Persistent homology provides one. A ball is grown around every code. When two balls touch, the two codes are joined. Loops appear, then fill in. A loop that survives over a long range of radii is real structure; one that appears and vanishes at once is noise.

Worked example. Six points on a regular hexagon of radius 1. Neighbours are 1 apart, every second point 1.73 apart.

The gap between two shapes is then measured on their diagrams:

persistence = death − birth · dB(D1, D2) = cost of the best matching between the points of two diagrams dB = 0 when the two point clouds have the same loops, of the same size

We compute the diagram of the latent codes of each model and compare it with the diagram of the true cylinder. All clouds are rescaled to a mean pairwise distance of 1, so a code space in 2 dimensions and data in 50 dimensions can be compared.

What is used, and what is not

Used: reproduce and measure

The authors' code, data generator, models and settings, pinned to a fixed version, for the cylinder experiment. Persistent homology and the bottleneck distance added as a topological check.

Not used

The paper's other experiments and the anchor loss, which fixes the orientation of the latent coordinates but not their topology.

The paper judges its latent spaces with reconstruction error and geodesic stress. Neither says directly whether the loop survived. Persistent homology does, which is why it is the added piece of this project.

My work

  1. Reproduce the cylinder experiment of the paper with the authors' code: Gaussian VAE against cylinder VAE.
  2. Add a topological evaluation of the latent spaces: persistence diagrams of the codes and bottleneck distance to the true cylinder.
  3. Repeat over several seeds and values of β, to test the paper's claim that the prescribed topology helps across the useful range of β.

Limitations

  • The cylinder VAE has a circle in its latent space by construction. Persistent homology therefore tests whether the codes cover the whole circle cleanly, not whether a circle exists.
  • One synthetic dataset with a known shape. Real data rarely announce their topology in advance.
  • The paper is a 2026 preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed.

Next steps

  • Vary β and the seeds, and plot reconstruction and loop size against β.
  • Explain one seed of the cylinder VAE whose geodesic stress is high while its loop is intact: the two measures disagree.
  • If time allows, a less synthetic case: rotated MNIST digits, where the rotation angle is the circle.
